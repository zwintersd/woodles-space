# marginalia — the 2.5d pass: an aquarium with a floor, and a shore to walk to

scope note: this is a design proposal, not a record of shipped work. it sits between
[DIORAMA_ROADMAP.md](./DIORAMA_ROADMAP.md) (the craft of `WorldCanvas.svelte` as it
stands) and [DESIGN.md](./DESIGN.md) (the systems the diorama displays). it proposes
**no change to the engine, the save format, or the balance numbers** — every mechanic
in DESIGN.md keeps its meaning, and `pnpm --filter marginalia balance` should report
identical figures either side of this work. what changes is what the player is looking
at when those mechanics run.

everything below cites the code it's grounded in. where i'm proposing rather than
describing, it says so.

---

## the pitch, in two sentences

the diorama already believes in a ground plane — it draws flattened ellipse shadows
under floor creatures, dims deeper sediment rows, and lays a bezier shelf-edge at the
floor line — but the geometry underneath is still a flat wall in elevation. and the
game's actual arc, *pour silt until the seabed breaks the surface and land appears*,
is currently depicted as a mode switch.

this pass makes both literal: one camera that reads as an aquarium seen in cutaway,
and one continuous world you can travel sideways along, from deep water to the shore
the silt built.

---

## part 0 — what the code already believes

### `y` is doing two jobs

a single scalar means *screen position* and *depth into the scene* at once, and the
renderer is quietly inconsistent about which:

| where | what it does | which reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `WORLD_WATER_TOP = 0.34` | flat line, sky above / water below | elevation |
| `drawSedimentGrid` | 48×12 cells onto a uniform rect, `cellH = waterH / grid.h` | elevation (a wall of silt) |
| `drawSedimentGrid` alpha | `(0.06 + value * 0.22) * (1 - depth * 0.18)` | **ground plane** (atmospheric depth) |
| `drawFeatures`, `drawCreatureLayers` | flattened ellipse shadows at `y + size * 0.28` | **ground plane** (shadows only make sense on a receding floor) |
| `drawShallowsShelf` | bezier edge at `FLOOR_TOP` | **ground plane** (that's a horizon) |

three of five already want a floor. this pass finishes the argument rather than
importing a new one.

### the shallows unlock is already the story

this is the finding that reshaped the whole document. from `TheWorld.svelte`:

```
if (book.sedimentCoverage >= SEDIMENT_UNLOCK_COVERAGE) return 'the floor has learned shallows.';
```

`SEDIMENT_UNLOCK_COVERAGE = 0.6`, and crossing it runs `unlockWorldspacesForCoverage`,
which opens `'shallows'` — the worldspace whose `visibleCategories` is `'all'` rather
than `['aquatic']`, and which is what `Waymarks.svelte` gates on when it says *"the
shallows need to open before she can mark the shore or sky."*

so the mechanic already reads: **she raises the floor until it meets the surface, and
land begins.** the renderer depicts that as a category filter widening and a shelf
gradient appearing. it is the most consequential thing that happens in world 1 and it
currently has no picture.

one correction to the mental image, from `world1/life.ts`: the terrestrial roster is
`lichen`, `moss`, `fungal_net`, `spring`. there is no fish with legs. the first thing
to live out of the water is *"a grey-gold crust on the bare rock"* that *"advances a
hair each year and unmakes the rock as it goes. the first soil is its leavings."* the
set piece in part 2 is designed for that, not for the genre image — it's slower, and
better.

### what's already right, and must survive

- **sprites are billboards.** every creature is drawn axis-aligned via `drawImage`,
  side-on. in an aquarium cutaway that is *correct*, not a compromise. the sprite
  library and the `CREATURE_SPECS` sheet convention carry over untouched.
- **the offscreen sediment bake.** `ensureSedimentBaked` invalidates on
  `(grid, W, H)`. the strategy survives; only its extent changes (part 4).
- **the spawn-point cache.** keyed `${activeWorldspace}:${spawnRevision}` — still the
  right key.
- **`reduce` is live**, re-read on OS change. perspective is not motion; only the
  camera is. reduced motion must collapse to a still, still-correct projection — never
  to a fallback renderer.

## part 1 — the camera: aquarium with a floor

### the decision

camera at roughly water level, tilted slightly down. sky stays a flat backdrop; the
water surface stays a line; the **floor recedes to a horizon inside the water**.

rejected: the tilted-tabletop camera (looking down at ~30°, the world as an object).
the pour reads best of any option there, but it kills the sky, invalidates every
side-on sprite in the library, and turns a diorama into a map.

### the coordinate change

`y` splits into **`z`** (depth into the scene, on the floor plane) and **`h`** (height
above it), with one new module:

```
project(x, z, h) -> { sx, sy, scale }
unproject(sx, sy) -> { x, z }   // replaces worldYToWaterGrid for pointer hit-testing
```

`waterGridYToWorld` / `worldYToWaterGrid` become the forward and inverse of this
projection rather than a linear remap. **placed features keep their stored `y`
meaning** (grid space) — they just project differently. no migration.

### the grid is already the axes you need

`SEDIMENT_GRID_W = 48` is already along-canvas x. `SEDIMENT_GRID_H = 12` is already
into-the-band. the only reinterpretation is that the 12 rows become **z into the
scene** rather than screen-y within the floor band. `w` needs no reinterpretation at
all.

### don't touch the grid dimensions

`SedimentGrid { w, h, cells }` lives inside `WorldShape`, which is persisted. changing
`h` from 12 to something finer owes a save migration.

12 rows is coarse for a receding plane — near-row trapezoids will be chunky —
so **bilinear-sample the 48×12 into a finer render grid at draw time**. free, no
migration, no balance impact, and the bake absorbs the cost once per change.

> **this lands with C, not A.** A draws the floor as soft scattered sprites and
> alpha-blended ellipses, which have no hard cell edges to look coarse — a far row
> that thins to ~3px reads as distance, not as a stripe. It's the *heightfield* in C
> that draws solid geometry per cell, and that's where subdivision starts earning its
> complexity. Deferred until then.

## part 2 — the lateral axis: scrolling to the shore

### the world is a continuum, not two modes

`Worldspace = 'water' | 'shallows'` (`types.ts:75`) is currently a scene swap.
proposal: **the two worldspaces become regions of one continuous world laid out along
x** — deep water at the left, shelving up to shore at the right — and the camera
travels between them.

`visibleCategories` then becomes literal instead of administrative: before the shallows
open, you cannot see terrestrial life *because you have not been where it lives*. the
unlock stops being a filter widening and becomes **a soft camera bound moving right**.

concretely: unlocking a worldspace changes what the camera may reach, not what data
exists. that is a renderer-side change; `world.setWorldspace` keeps its current job.

### sea level is flat; the floor rises to meet it

this is the load-bearing idea. today `WORLD_WATER_TOP = 0.34` is a screen constant.
it should become **a world constant — sea level — at a fixed height in world space**,
with the terrain varying underneath it:

```
floorHeight(x, z) = bathymetry(x) + siltRise(x, z)
```

- **`bathymetry(x)`** is authored: deep at the left, shelving up toward the right.
  this is what gives the shore a fixed *place* rather than letting it appear anywhere.
- **`siltRise(x, z)`** comes straight from the sediment grid cell value — the thing the
  pour already writes.

the shoreline is then **derived, never authored**: it's wherever `floorHeight` crosses
sea level. pouring silt at the right end of the world literally moves the waterline,
and `sedimentCoverage` crossing 0.6 is the moment the shelf breaks the surface
somewhere. the unlock copy — *"the floor has learned shallows"* — becomes a caption for
something visible.

**balance is untouched by this.** `sedimentCoverage` is a fraction of cells over the
threshold; it does not care how wide the world those cells describe is. the unlock
economy (`SEDIMENT_UNLOCK_COST = 60`, coverage 0.6) is unchanged.

**existing saves change appearance, not data.** a silt pattern poured against a
one-screen world will read as spread across a wider one. nothing is lost; it will look
different. worth a line in the release note, not a migration.

### the cutaway is the whole trick

when the camera reaches the shore, do you see *under* the water or only its surface?

**take the cutaway.** the water is a glass wall; you always see below the line. it's
the aquarium reading held consistently, and it is the only version where the set piece
below is legible in a single frame — you watch the shelf rise through the surface in
section, the way a geology diagram would draw it.

rejected: a reflective surface read from land. prettier in isolation, but it means the
camera loses the underwater half exactly when the underwater half is the point, and it
would force two incompatible water renderers.

### what `x` means now

spawn-point `x` becomes a world coordinate rather than a canvas fraction. same numbers,
wider meaning — and the authored values want re-spacing once they mean this:

| point | `x` today | reads as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `surface-drift` | 0.20 | open water, left |
| `middle-current` | 0.52 | mid |
| `deep-blue` | 0.80 | currently "right", should be **deep**, i.e. left |
| `first-shelf` (shore) | 0.38 | should sit **right**, at the shelf |
| `salt-mist-line` (air) | 0.70 | air over the shore — right is correct |

that's a content edit in `worldShape.ts`, not a mechanism change, but it must happen in
the same pass or the layout will read as scrambled.

### the air environment

past the waterline the frame composition inverts: water shrinks, sky fills. the depth
cues have to swap with it — underwater haze is dense and tinted; air haze is thin and
pale.

do it as **one fog function with two coefficient sets, lerped by how much of the column
at that x is water.** the transition is then automatic and continuous, with no seam to
hand-author, and it means the `layer: 'air'` spawns (`layerBob` amp `0.035`, the largest
of the three) finally have somewhere distinct to be: mist over deep water at the left,
birds-over-the-shore at the right.

### the set piece

`lichen` requires `['reaching', 'holding']`. when it first emerges, the world should
show you where:

1. the camera pans right of its own accord, along the shelf the player raised.
2. it settles where `floorHeight` crosses sea level — bare rock, just above the line.
3. the lichen's `notice` copy lands: *"a grey-gold crust on the bare rock."*

no new mechanic. an emergence already fires; the camera just has an opinion about it
now. and the pan is only possible because the shore is *somewhere* rather than a mode.

### parallax finally earns itself

in a static frame, parallax is a subtle polish at 960×480. with a lateral camera it's
the primary depth cue and nearly free: sky drifts slowest, far floor rows slower than
near ones, all falling out of `project()` and the camera's x. this is the pitch-1 flourish
that only pays once there's a scroll axis to pay it — which is a good argument for doing
the lateral work in the same arc rather than after.

## part 3 — the depth cues, ranked by payoff

1. **scale from depth.** `SpawnPoint.scale` exists (0.74–1.15) but is *authored*. derive
   it from z. `CREATURE_BOX = 0.2` must stop being a fixed fraction of `H`. cheapest
   change, largest effect: the world acquires size.
2. **atmospheric tint.** partly present already (the deep-row dimming; the water
   gradient darkening down). generalized, it gives a *principled* reason for every color
   stop — you choose a water color and a fog falloff instead of hand-tuning twelve
   gradients. **this subsumes the open color-science item in DIORAMA_ROADMAP.md**; those
   two should be reordered so the palette pass happens *after* this, on top of it,
   rather than before it and then again.
3. **z-sorting.** the real refactor. today the draw loop is four hand-ordered buckets:
   `drawCreatureLayers(['water','floor'])` → glaze → ripples → `(['shore','air'])`.
   2.5d wants: collect drawables → sort by z → draw. not hard, but it obsoletes the
   painter's-order reasoning and forces `drawWaterGlaze` / `drawWaterRipples` to have an
   actual z rather than a position in a list.
4. **parallax.** see above — free once the camera moves, near-invisible before.

## part 4 — the floor as a heightfield

draw each sediment cell as a trapezoid whose **near edge is taller than its far edge**
and whose **top edge lifts with density**. silt gains thickness; pouring builds a mound
visible in profile; a settled `shell_bed` sits *in* a hollow instead of on a texture.

baking strategy under a lateral camera: **bake the world-width floor once, blit a
window.** the invalidation key gains world extent and loses viewport dependence, which
is cleaner than today. size is not a concern — three screens of a floor band at 960×480
logical is on the order of 5760×192 device pixels at dpr 2.

## part 5 — movement and input

### the friction, named

**horizontal drag is already the pour gesture** (`startPour` / `movePour`, with
`setPointerCapture`). horizontal drag is also the obvious scroll gesture. they cannot
both be the default.

options:

| approach | pro | con |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pour only while armed (`canPourSediment()`) | zero new ui; the affordance already gates it | silently changes what a drag does depending on insight |
| pour as an explicit tool/mode | unambiguous; makes room for future tools | a mode is a thing to explain, and the game explains little on purpose |
| scroll on a separate rail (edge zones, wheel, keys) | never conflicts | edge-zone scrolling is fussy on touch |

**recommendation: pour as an explicit tool**, with scroll as the default drag. it costs
one small control, and it's the only option that stays legible when a second spatial
tool arrives — which it will, the moment waymarks get click-to-place.

### the camera's own motions

- **action-pan** — the camera travels to what she just did (the lichen set piece). the
  best fit for the game's grammar: *movement earned by attention*. she doesn't walk;
  the world turns toward what she tended.
- **action-dolly** — pushes in while pouring, drifts back after.
- **breathing sway** — slow always-on drift, same family as the existing `wave` term.
- **free scroll** — one axis, drag or wheel or arrow keys.

### accessibility

- **keyboard scroll and keyboard pour are both required.** a perspective floor makes
  "where am i pouring" harder to judge without a pointer; arrow keys moving a cursor
  over the grid fixes it — and incidentally kills the blind-slider waymark placement
  DIORAMA_ROADMAP.md already flags as debt. two problems, one input mode.
- **`reduce` must kill camera motion only.** projection, scale, and fog stay. an
  action-pan becomes an instant cut.
- **the `aria-label` needs the camera in it.** it currently reflects
  `book.canPourSediment()`; it should also say where in the world she's looking, since
  that is now state a sighted player can see and a screen-reader user cannot.
- **jump-to-region controls** (deep water / the shelf / the shore) so travel is never
  only a free-scroll grind.

## part 6 — what breaks

| site | today | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `WATER_TOP` | screen constant `0.34` | sea level in world space; waterline derived from `floorHeight` |
| bottom-edge clamps (`maxCy = H - dh * 0.65`) | stop deep floor spawns falling off the bottom | mostly dissolve — "deep" now means *far*, i.e. small and near the horizon. near-plane clamps replace them |
| `CREATURE_BOX = 0.2` | fixed fraction of `H` × authored scale | derived from z |
| grounding shadows at `cy + dh * 0.35` | decoration | land on the projected plane; become a real depth cue and deserve more care |
| `drawSedimentCast` | stream drawn at a **seeded x off `book.worldIndex`** — `pourPoint` is never consulted, so the silt doesn't fall where your finger is | originates at the surface above the projected pour point, falls into depth, lands on the mound it's raising |
| `drawShallowsShelf` | gradient wash gated on `activeWorldspace === 'shallows'` | deleted — the shelf is geometry now |
| `layerBob` | amplitude by layer | amplitude by layer **and** z, or near creatures will bob too little |
| draw loop | four hand-ordered layer buckets | z-sorted drawable list |

`drawSedimentCast` is the one i'd fix first regardless of whether the rest of this
happens. cause, travel, and effect through space, in one gesture, is the entire pitch in
miniature — and right now the middle term is missing.

## part 7 — sequence

each step is shippable on its own and leaves the app in a coherent state.

| | what | delta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** ✅ | `project()` / `unproject()`; floor rows reinterpreted as depth. no camera motion, no lateral axis. | biggest single visual jump |
| **B** ✅ | z-sorted draw list; scale and fog derived from z | the world gains size |
| **C** ✅ | pour originates at the pour point; silt mounds rise in profile | the mechanic finally reads |
| **D** ⚠️ | lateral axis: `bathymetry(x)`, derived waterline, world-space spawn x, worldspaces as regions, air/water fog lerp | **blocked — see "the two horizons"** |
| **E** | camera: free scroll, breathing, action-dolly, action-pan + the lichen set piece. reduced-motion and keyboard complete. | it becomes a world |

**A shipped.** `projection.ts` owns the plane; `waterGridYToWorld` /
`worldYToWaterGrid` became its forward and inverse, which moved the sediment grid,
placed features, feature auras, floor-layer life and decorative creatures onto it
with no change at their call sites. The two endpoint guarantees held, so
`worldShape.test.ts`'s existing assertions passed untouched — the floor still spans
exactly the band it spanned before. Measured: the near row of the 12-row grid is now
5.3× the screen height of the far row (17.8px vs 3.4px at H=480), the far edge spans
73% of the width, and a sprite at the far edge draws at 40%. Verified live that a
pour still lands where the pointer says: at the near edge screen x 0.20 hits column 9
of 48, and at the far edge the same screen column reaches column 5, because the far
row's narrower screen span covers a wider stretch of world.

One thing changed that wasn't purely geometric: the sediment's distance dimming used
to fade the *near* rows, which was the flat-wall reading. On a plane it's the far rows
that recede, so that term is inverted. B replaces it with real depth fog anyway.

**B shipped.** The water column had no depth axis to sort or fade by — a spawn point
there stores `(x, y)` where y is its layer's *band*, i.e. height, not distance. Rather
than add a persisted field, each point takes a stable depth from its own id
(`sceneDepthFromSeed`), so the volume is populated deterministically, distinct per
point, across frames and reloads, with no save change. The column occupies the near end
of the floor's own axis (`SCENE_FAR_Z = 0.35`), so one foreshortening function serves
the whole scene — a creature at the back of the water reads at the same scale the floor
does at that distance, which is the entire point of having one camera.

Fog is Beer-Lambert falloff normalized so the far wall lands exactly on `FOG_MAX`, and
its color is sampled down the water's own gradient — a far creature near the surface
hazes pale, one near the floor hazes blue — rather than introducing a second palette.
It replaced the floor's A-era placeholder dimming, so floor and water now answer to the
same function. Tinting happens on the sprite's own pixels via a reused scratch canvas
and `source-atop`, sized to where the sprite *lands* rather than to the source art: a
bound Bestiary creature can be a 1024px png drawn at 90.

Sorting is per-pass, not global. The four hand-ordered buckets became two passes split
at the water's surface, because the glaze and ripples are a film *on* it rather than an
object *in* it and have no honest z. Within each pass the previous order was
`book.life`'s roster order, which had nothing to do with distance — a creature at the
back could paint over one at the front. One ordering change beyond that: the animator
swimmer now draws after both the life and the decorative creatures in its pass rather
than between them, which suits it, since it's a witch-influence effect rather than a
resident.

Measured on 200-frame samples in a software-rendered container: at a realistic scene
(12 animated creatures) B and A-only are indistinguishable, both at a 50.0ms median. At
48 creatures — four times any real scene — B lands on 66.6ms in one run of two against
A-only's 50.0. Frame gaps there quantize to vsync multiples, so that is one extra
quantum occasionally, not a measured cost curve; on GPU-backed canvas the scratch path
is the cheap case. Worth re-measuring on real hardware before C adds geometry.

**C shipped.** `drawSedimentCast` now takes the landing spot: the stream starts at the
surface above where the pointer actually resolved to, falls to it, and puffs where it
arrives — foreshortened by the landing depth, so a pour at the back of the plane is a
shorter, narrower fall than one at the front. Cause, travel, effect, through space, in
one gesture. `projectFloor` grew a height argument, so silt stands proud of the plane,
and everything anchored to the floor rides the silt it stands on rather than the bare
plane beneath it — the pour cursor, placed features (`placeFeatureOnBestSediment` seeks
exactly the deep cells that now stand up), their auras, and the scatter.

The surface is drawn as depth bands, far edge first, each one's top following the
interpolated height. Painter's order does the occluding. Two things had to vary *along*
each band, both via horizontal gradients, which is the only way to vary either across a
single fill:

- **opacity by local density, not the band's peak.** The first version used the peak and
  the seabed rendered as a slab — one tall mound painting its whole row at mound
  opacity.
- **lightness by slope.** A field of smooth mounds has almost no silhouette from a
  camera this low, and 18 near-parallel crest lines merge into a wash rather than a
  profile. What makes relief legible here is shading: the face descending toward the
  viewer catches light, the back of the same mound falls away. That, not outline, is
  what made the mounds read.

The first version also looped front-to-back, which defeats the occlusion the whole
approach rests on.

**Subdivision landed here, smaller than part 1 assumed.** 96×36 and 64×18 are visually
indistinguishable — the extra samples interpolate detail the persisted 12 rows never
held — so the source grid, not the screen, is what bounds useful subdivision.

**And C cost real frames, which is worth recording.** `applySedimentPour` returns a new
grid every tick, so the bake re-runs every frame of a live pour, and C made that bake a
substantial piece of work. Measured as frames delivered in a fixed 3s window (software
rendering, so read the deltas, not the absolutes):

| | idle | pouring |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B | 48–49 | 44–48 |
| C, first cut (96×36) | — | 28–29 |
| C, shipped (64×18) | 50–57 | 39–43 |

A ~40% loss on the app's central interaction, cut to ~10%. Coarser gradient stops helped
a little; the resolution was the real cost. There is also a 90ms floor on re-baking
during a pour — the grid still updates every frame, so the pour's arithmetic is
untouched and only the painted floor trails it, under a stream drawn live on top, with
the frame the pour ends forced so what she let go of is what she sees. That throttle
does **not** bind in this container, where frames are already further apart than 90ms;
it is there for real hardware, and is the first thing to verify on it.

**D is half shipped and half blocked, and the blocker is the interesting part.**

Shipped: the seabed has a shape. `bathymetryRise(x, coverage)` gives it a smoothstep
shelf — deep on the left, shallowing to the right — added inside `projectFloor`, so
every floor-anchored thing rides it with no change at its call site, the way A moved
them all onto the plane by changing one function. The surface renderer draws bands as
strips between two sampled profiles rather than a crest over a flat baseline, since the
floor now varies in x as well as z. The shelf rises with `sedimentCoverage`, so the
floor she fills is the floor that shallows.

The inverse got harder and is the piece worth reading: bathymetry couples the axes — the
seabed's height depends on x, and which x a screen column means depends on z — so the
one-step solve from A no longer works. Given a rise it is still closed form, so
`floorDepthAt` iterates "guess the depth, read off x, take that column's rise, solve
again" and then validates by projecting back. That round-trip is now what decides
whether a screen point is on the seabed at all, which a flat band never had to ask.

### the two horizons

**Land cannot emerge from this floor without breaking the scene, and that is a
composition problem, not a number.**

The seabed recedes to a vanishing point at `FLOOR_HORIZON_Y` = 0.667. The water is drawn
as a flat line at `SEA_LEVEL_Y` = 0.340 — a third of the frame *above* it. Terrain is
allowed to rise above a horizon, but to get from one to the other the near edge of the
shelf has to ramp across 66% of the frame height. Built and looked at: it reads as a wall
thrown across the diorama, not as a beach.

The cause is that the scene has two cameras and always has. A water line at 0.34 with sky
above it means the eye is at the water's surface, which puts the floor's vanishing point
at 0.34 too. A chose `FLOOR_FOCAL` to preserve the original floor band and landed the
horizon at 0.667 instead. Nothing exposed the disagreement while the floor was the only
thing in perspective. A shore exposes it immediately.

Three ways out, with what each costs:

| | what | cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. one camera** | re-derive so the floor's horizon *is* sea level | `FLOOR_FOCAL` drops ~1.5 → ~0.43. Far-edge scale goes 0.40 → 0.70, so most of A–C's depth flattens. Consistent, and the shore just works. |
| **2. move the water** | drop the drawn surface to the floor's horizon | sky becomes the top two-thirds and the water a shallow strip. The diorama becomes a different picture. |
| **3. no shore here** | the shelf shallows, land waits for a camera that can travel to it | keeps A–C intact and ships today. Gives up the fish-out-of-water image until E. |

**Taken 3 for now**, because it is the only one that does not trade shipped, verified work
for unverified work, and because E's camera may make the question moot: a camera that can
travel does not need the shore to fit in the same frame as the deep water. Everything
option 1 or 2 would need — the profile, the coverage coupling, land shading, the coupled
inverse — is written and working. Two constants (`SHELF_BASE`, `SHELF_COVERAGE_LIFT`) hold
it under water, and tests pin that as deliberate so it fails loudly rather than drifting.

Not done, and waiting on the same decision: worldspaces as regions, world-space spawn `x`,
and the air/water fog lerp, which has nothing to lerp between while there is no air side.

A is a weekend and is worth doing even if the arc stops there. C is where it changes how
the game feels to play. D is where it changes what the game is *about* — and it's the
one that pays back the shallows unlock the design has been carrying unillustrated since
it shipped.

## forks — decisions this document doesn't get to make

1. **how wide is the world?** i've assumed a fixed extent, with the 48 grid columns
   spanning all of it and worldspaces as regions within — because that needs no save
   migration and no change to coverage math. the alternative, a world that grows
   rightward as worldspaces unlock, is more dramatic and costs a migration.
2. **does the pour become a tool?** part 5's recommendation adds one control to an app
   that is deliberately sparse with controls. worth pushing back on if the sparseness
   matters more than the second gesture.
3. **does `bathymetry(x)` vary per world?** authored per world is simplest and best for
   world 1. procedural is a DESIGN.md phase-E concern and shouldn't be designed for yet,
   but the function signature should leave room.
4. **does the camera ever leave the water?** everything above keeps the cutaway, so the
   camera stays at sea level and the land is seen in section from the sea's side. a
   camera that climbs above the shore to look *down* at the land is a second camera, and
   i'd rather ship one.
